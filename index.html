01 · About

Akwaaba!

Akwaaba means welcome in Ghana. I am an Assistant Professor of Business Administration (Data Science) at the Williams School of Commerce, Economics and Politics at Washington and Lee University, where I teach business analytics and data visualization. My research examines entrepreneurial legitimacy on digital platforms and how it manifests across different contexts.

Much of my work is set in crowdfunding and digital entrepreneurship, with particular attention to emerging markets. I study how entrepreneurs establish legitimacy with backers they will never meet, how ventures raise capital where traditional financial institutions fall short, and how platforms designed around Western assumptions interact with communities that have their own traditions of collective fundraising. That interest motivated my research on Harambee, the Kenyan practice of community fundraising, and a broader argument that digital finance should be designed around local values rather than imported ones. Though information systems is my home discipline, my work crosses into entrepreneurship, marketing, and sociology, and I regularly collaborate across those fields. This research has appeared in leading journals in information systems and entrepreneurship, including the Journal of the Association for Information Systems, the Information Systems Journal, and the Journal of Business Venturing Insights, and has established, among other findings, that value alignment between founders and backers is a significant driver of campaign success.

My current research examines legitimacy in the age of AI. I ask whether artificial intelligence can broaden access to capital for underrepresented founders and how exaggerated AI claims affect the trust that backers place in campaigns. Evidence from Kickstarter and Indiegogo indicates that campaigns invoking AI capabilities are funded at lower rates, a result that runs counter to established findings in the crowdfunding literature. This ongoing work is funded by the 2026 Mednick Memorial Fellowship from the Virginia Foundation for Independent Colleges.

I grew up in Ghana, where I also earned my undergraduate degree, before completing my doctorate in the United States. That trajectory informs both my research agenda and my teaching. This spring I co-taught "Tech for Good," a Spring Term Abroad course that brought W&L students to Ghana to examine how technology interacts with local institutions and trust, and I am co-editing a special issue on alternative finance in Africa. Away from work, I am a Christian, a husband, and father to a beautiful little girl. I love soccer and basketball, both playing and watching.

Journal Publications

The Paradoxical Impact of Smartphone Addiction on Innovative Performance: A Conservation of Resources Theory Perspective

Moqbel, M., Frimpong, B., & Zhang, L. — Information Systems Frontiers, 2026

Smartphone addiction is usually assumed to hurt performance at work. We find a more surprising story: it drains the energy and focus employees need to innovate, yet it also keeps them connected, and that connectivity feeds innovative performance. The harm and the benefit coexist.

Achieving Reward-based Crowdfunding Project Success: An Examination of Value Congruence

Frimpong, B., Ayaburi, E., & Andoh-Baidoo, F. K. — Journal of the Association for Information Systems, 2025

We analyze the language of thousands of campaigns to measure how closely the values founders express match those of their backers. Alignment helps, but only up to a point, and only when founders still sound like the community they are pitching to.

Harambee as a Decolonial Digital Fundraising Approach

Frimpong, B., Ayaburi, E., & Andoh-Baidoo, F. K. — Information Systems Journal, 2025

Positions Harambee, the Kenyan tradition of communal fundraising, as a decolonial alternative to Western platform logics. Shows how indigenous users repurpose digital tools to balance cultural expectations with technological affordances, and argues digital finance should be designed around local communal values rather than imported ones.

Impact of Campaign Bundling on the Success of Donation-Based Crowdfunding

Frimpong, B., Ayaburi, E., Andoh-Baidoo, F. K., Wang, X., & Xiao, N. — International Journal of Electronic Commerce, 2024

Campaign bundling pools contributions across projects in a community-pot style that is counterintuitive to the competitive, winner-takes-backing logic of crowdfunding. Yet we show bundling is effective, improving success in donation-based crowdfunding rather than splitting donor attention.
ABDC AABS 3
Take My Word for It! The Role of Projected Certainty Signaling and Certainty Alignment in Reward Crowdfunding Outcomes

Frimpong, B., Mohammed, F., Larson, M. K., & Anderson, H. J. — Journal of Business Venturing Insights, 2024

Founders who sound certain raise more money, but not directly. Projected certainty works through its alignment with the certainty backers themselves express, and it is that alignment that carries the effect on funding outcomes.

Innovation Choices in Modern Times: The Interplay of Technology and Nontechnology Approaches

Co-taught with sociologist Jon Eastwood.
